# Self-Study Archive

**Foundations in Mathematics, Physics, and Computer Science**

This repository is a disciplined, version-controlled archive of my independent coursework. It is structured to systematically build a rigorous theoretical foundation, targeting the knowledge equivalent of an undergraduate education in mathematics, physics, and computer science through open-source curricula from MIT, Stanford, and Yale.

---

## The Problem Statement

Standard academic progression and time-bound competitive engineering environments often prioritize pattern matching, speed, and algorithmic execution over deep, fundamental comprehension. While this builds sharp problem-solving mechanics, it leaves a distinct gap in theoretical understanding. 

Attempting to bridge this gap through ad-hoc self-study—watching disparate videos or reading isolated articles—often leads to a fragmented, shallow knowledge base. Without the rigid structure, sequential problem sets, and demanding syllabi of top-tier university environments, self-taught knowledge remains superficial.

## The Solution

This repository serves as a centralized accountability mechanism and execution environment. Rather than passively consuming educational content, I am treating this self-directed curriculum with the exact same discipline as a formal university degree. 

By strictly adhering to the exact course structures provided by MIT OpenCourseWare, Stanford, and Yale, this workspace transitions my focus from surface-level application to first-principles mastery. It tracks my progress through formal problem sets, mathematical proofs, and computational simulations.

## Repository Architecture

The workspace is intentionally categorized by university course codes to maintain academic structure and prevent knowledge silos. 

*   **Mathematics (MIT 18.xx Series):** Focuses on transitioning from computational math to pure math. Includes Single/Multi-Variable Calculus (18.01, 18.02), Differential Equations (18.03), Linear Algebra (18.06), Discrete Mathematics (18.310), and the foundational bridge into rigorous proofs via Real Analysis (18.100A).
*   **Physics (MIT 8.xx Series & Yale):** Establishes classical foundations before moving into modern physics. Tracks progression from Yale's Fundamentals of Physics to MIT's advanced modules, including Waves and Vibrations (8.03), Electricity and Magnetism, and Quantum Physics I+II (8.04/8.05).
*   **Computer Science & Artificial Intelligence (MIT 6.xx & Stanford CS):** Spans theoretical algorithms (MIT 6.006, 6.046J) to advanced, graduate-level machine learning architectures. Includes Stanford's comprehensive AI sequence: Machine Learning (CS229), Computer Vision (CS231n), NLP (CS224n), and Language Modeling (CS336).
*   **Intuition Building:** Incorporates supplementary models (like 3blue1brown) to establish geometric and visual intuition before tackling dense algebraic proofs.

## Methodology

Passive consumption of lectures creates an illusion of competence. The core philosophy of this repository is active learning and sequential problem-solving. 

This workspace is not a collection of lecture notes. It houses the actual work: computational models, algorithmic implementations, and coded solutions to the rigorous problem sets assigned in these courses. The goal is to build muscle memory in recognizing mathematical patterns and structuring logical proofs.
